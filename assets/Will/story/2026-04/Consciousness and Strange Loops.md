## I. Introduction: The Hard Problem of Consciousness

David Chalmers directly confronted a taboo topic within the cognitive science community by bringing up the following question: Why does subjective experience exist at all?

He termed it as the “hard problem of consciousness” to explicitly set it apart from what he called the “easy” questions regarding functional concerns like vision, memory, attention, etc. that neuroscience is at least theoretically well equipped to answer. While science has progressed in explaining how the brain works, the deeper question persists: Why and how is any of this physical neural processing accompanied by an inner “felt” experience? Why is there something phenomenological of what it is like to personally taste chocolate, that seems to exist beyond just functional explanation?

Much of the debate surrounding consciousness has been about the question itself. Philosophers and scientists have been talking past each other for decades in part because the Hard Problem bundles together questions that require different kinds of responses. In this paper, I will first reduce ambiguity around the topic by sharpening what is actually being asked. Throughout, I will mainly focus on perspectives from two leading philosophers of mind: David Chalmers who coined the problem, and Daniel Dennett who rejected it. Once we pull these apart, I will then propose my own theory for how to think about consciousness, one that I believe can be compatible with both panpsychist and physicalist perspectives. Finally, I will apply my framework in response to three prevailing wonders about consciousness that I assume anyone interested in the topic has asked themselves before.

While our intuitions about reality are typically wrong, some of our deepest considerations about consciousness that seem to resist logical explanation might actually be pointing at something structurally important (even if they are pointing at it for the wrong reasons). So rather than dismissing those intuitions outright, I want to offer a theory that takes them seriously and attempts a resolution. The framework I propose is speculative and underdeveloped given the constraints of this paper, but I believe it is consistent and potentially falsifiable, assuming our scientific models and instruments become more complete and accurate over time. At the very least, I think it is just a fun way to think about consciousness.

## II. The Current Debate

To set up the premise for my paper, we begin with an observation that the Hard Problem is really asking two separate questions. The first is: Why is there even experience at all? And the second is: How does experience arise from physical functions? These are two separate things. The first question is one I believe science is not designed to answer. While it can try to explain what things are and how they work, it cannot tell you why anything exists in the first place. It is an existential question that sits in the same family of “why does the universe exist?”, or “why is there something, rather than nothing?”. The reason I want to add this clarification is because some debates surrounding the Hard Problem conflate existential questions with functional ones and end up denying the real problem. Narrowing in on the second question of “how”, then makes it a valid question to identify what is necessary for conscious experience. It is this question that I will be focusing on for the rest of my paper.

### 2.1 Chalmers: The Problem Is Real

David Chalmers argued that consciousness is a problem unlike any other. You can explain every cognitive function of the brain and still be left with an unanswered question: How do cognitive functions feel like something? His conclusion was that a reductive explanation in neuroscience was structurally unable to close this gap. Functions do not logically entail experience, so he believed a new kind of theory was needed, and one that may need to treat consciousness as irreducible and a fundamental part of physics. This is the essence of panpsychism: the theory that consciousness, like mass and charge, is just an inherent property of all matter.

### 2.2 Dennett: The Problem Is a Chimera

Daniel Dennett rejected the problem entirely and thought the Hard Problem was an illusion. He argued that individual features of consciousness are not mysterious. For instance, the experience of color, the feeling of joy, or the sense of love, each corresponds to a cognitive function that neuroscience can, in principle, explain. The problem only seemed hard because we lumped these functions together under a mysterious label of “consciousness” and convinced ourselves that some grand mystery must be underlying the whole package.

To support this, Dennett drew an analogy to vitalism. In the early days, biologists believed that life required a special “life force”, an élan vital, beyond ordinary chemistry. However, once advances in biochemistry explained metabolism, replication, heredity, etc., that belief simply dissolved. Dennett claimed consciousness will follow the same vitalism trajectory: explain all the functions, and the residual mystery evaporates.

Because I think Chalmers raised a real question, my response will be countering Dennett’s. Here, I want to use his vitalism analogy to argue the opposite. Vitalism dissolved the “easy” mechanistic questions of how living systems work, but the deeper question of how inanimate matter becomes animate in the first place was not answered by those same advances. The philosophy of abiogenesis remains unsolved. Vitalism being disproven does not suggest that there are no further questions within life formation; what it did was allow for scientists to better categorize and understand the mechanisms of life. So I think Dennett’s analogy proves less than he needs it to: it only demonstrates that mechanistic mysteries dissolve once the mechanisms are explained. Neuroscience is doing for cognition what biochemistry did for the mechanisms of life — explaining the functional machinery. It is not progress on why any of this machinery is accompanied by experience, just as explaining the formation of a cell is not progress on why certain configurations of matter are alive at all.

In “Consciousness Cannot Be Separated from Function”, Cohen and Dennett sharpen their deflationary stance to argue that any theory positing phenomenal experience independent of cognitive access (what they call “dissociative” theories), is unfalsifiable by design and therefore falls outside the bounds of science. To this, they consider a thought experiment: imagine severing every connection between the brain’s color-processing regions and the rest of the brain, then asking the patient what they see. The patient would report seeing an apple but with no color whatsoever. According to dissociative theories, the patient should still be phenomenally conscious of red, since the neural conditions for color consciousness are fully met. But the patient denies any color experience and the dissociative theorist is forced to claim the patient is conscious of red but simply cannot know it. If even this perfect experiment cannot falsify the theory, Cohen and Dennett argue that nothing can. This argument assumes that the only way to verify the patient’s experience of red is through the very cognitive access channels that have been severed. However, the inability to report an experience is not evidence of its absence, it is evidence that our current method of detection depends entirely on functional access, which is exactly what has been removed. The thought experiment, then, does not prove that consciousness cannot exist without function. It proves that we cannot currently detect consciousness without cognitive function.

I think consciousness falls into a category of questions that are “unfalsifiable” only given our current tools and understanding today. A claim can be temporarily untestable because our instruments are not yet sophisticated enough to address it. Atomic theory was unfalsifiable for decades before scanning tunneling microscopes existed to confirm it. Gravitational waves were predicted a hundred years before LIGO detected them. In neither case would it have been correct to declare the question unscientific simply because it could not yet be tested. For instance, in the Paleolithic times, the statement “invisible living things are making us sick” could have been claimed to be unfalsifiable because there was no possible way to ever see “invisible things”. Fast forward a couple thousand years, and our definition of “see” has now expanded to include observables made possible by the microscope, allowing us to uncover the “invisible” bacteria that can cause disease. So while the statement “we can only access consciousness through cognitive function and you cannot falsify phenomenal experience beyond what function reveals” is true for now, there is no reason to think that it will remain that way as science progresses.

### 2.3 Can We Redefine “Physical”?

Before moving on, I want to elaborate my stance that I do think the basis of consciousness will still be “physical” in the sense that it will turn out to be something grounded in physics. But that does not mean it needs to be explained by neuroscience alone. It also does not mean it needs to be explained by current physics yet. The models we have today are incomplete, and we will expand our vocabulary when we discover new things. With that said, I think neurons and cognitive functions are sufficient for consciousness, but not necessary for it.

One could argue that I am just stretching the definition of “physical” to cover for what we mean by “non-physical” by today’s standards. But every major expansion in science has looked this way from the prior framework. Electromagnetism’s invisible fields seemed non-physical under the Newtonian model. Before Einstein, space was an empty void until he proved that spacetime itself is a fabric that warps in response to mass. Quantum mechanics forced scientists to expand their definition of physical reality from definite, localized positions to superposition and entanglement. In each case, “physical” turned out to include more than anyone expected. I believe the problem of consciousness may be less of a neuroscience problem, and more of one that lives at the intersection of physics and the study of information organization itself.

## III. My Theory of Consciousness

What follows is very speculative, but it’s a way to think of the “how” question that I believe addresses some core challenges of the hard problem. I write about it because it may offer solutions surrounding prevailing mysteries of “emergence”, the privacy of subjective experience, and the “feltness” of consciousness that aligns with our intuition. The biggest question would then be, if we can, at least in principle, evaluate it. If we try to look for consciousness not only in the brain but within matter itself, perhaps we can.

> Consciousness is an intrinsic property of matter, but one that exists in a dormant state only activated when a specific structural condition is met: self-referential recursion.

In this view, consciousness does not depend on what a system is, but what a system is doing. This framework emphasizes two hypotheses:

> **Hypothesis (1):** Consciousness is a fundamental but dormant property embedded in physical matter

Subjective experience can then be understood as what occurs when the dormant consciousness is activated by some specific condition. The potential is universal, but the expression is conditional. An imperfect analogy is gene expression, where every cell contains the complete human genome but the vast majority of those genes are dormant, only activating when specific transcription factors bind. While classical panpsychism says that everything is conscious to some degree (that there is “something it is like” to be an electron), this hypothesis restricts that possibility.

> **Hypothesis (2):** Self-Referential Recursion is the Activation Condition

The activation condition is self-referential recursive information processing. This is the point at which the organization of a system becomes complex enough to model its own states. The threshold is not merely information processing, but actively constructing an internal representation of itself as a system that is processing information. For example, a thermometer responds to temperature, but does not represent itself responding to temperature, so it is not conscious. The activation is a binary condition: at any given time, the system is either modelling its own processing or it is not. Once it is, then consciousness becomes a spectrum: richer self-models yield richer experience. This self-reference idea parallels Douglas Hofstadter’s concept of the “strange loop” in Gödel, Escher, Bach and I Am a Strange Loop. Hofstadter argued that the self emerges when a system’s symbolic activity becomes rich enough to twist back upon itself. The “I” is not something sitting inside the brain, it’s a pattern when the brain’s activity traces refers to itself.

One might wonder: why not just go directly with Hofstadter’s account and skip the panpsychist framing? Hofstadter’s idea, by itself, faces the same objection that all functionalist approaches face: it explains the structure and dynamics of self-reference, but still doesn’t explain how that structure has an experiential character. It regresses back to the initial problem of how the loop would “feel” like anything at all. Hypothesis (1) addresses that vulnerability, it being, that the self-referential structure does not generate experience from nothing, but rather that it activates a potential that was already present in the physical substrate.

Similarly, one could also ask: why not skip the self-referential recursion and just consider the classical panpsychist framing? A common critique of panpsychism is that it does not explain why different mini-experiences form together into one coherent sense of self (the combination problem). This is a valid question: if consciousness is fundamental to all matter, then why should the brain produce a single unified experience rather than billions of disconnected experiences? Hypothesis (2) resolves this problem. There are no mini-experiences to combine because the consciousness potential does not manifest at all until the activation condition is met. A self-referential system does not model each of its components separately and then stitch those models together; it models itself as a singular, unified system. So while the combination problem assumes that consciousness begins at the bottom and is aggregated upward, this framework assumes consciousness begins at the level of the system.

## IV. Three Wonders of Consciousness

Here, I will apply my framework to address three puzzling questions of consciousness. The first asks, what really counts as conscious and nonconscious? The second asks, why is consciousness privately inaccessible? And the third asks, why does consciousness “feel” like it floats above physical machinery?

### 4.1 The Categorization Problem

There is a practical observation that some things appear to be conscious and others do not. Rocks are probably not conscious. Dogs seem to be conscious. But where exactly can we draw the line? This is a hard problem in itself because we have no direct access to any consciousness other than our own. This is the problem of other minds: that is, we can never truly know whether anything outside ourselves is conscious. My dormant consciousness idea does not attempt to solve this problem; what it offers instead is a structural criterion for distinguishing systems that are candidates for experiencing, from those that are not experiencing, similar to how we can categorize most things as living or nonliving.

However, until physics finds something like dormant consciousness in particles, Hypothesis (1) is perhaps a leap of faith by today’s standards. Hypothesis (2), on the other hand, provides an organizational condition that scientists can actually look for today. The question shifts from “does this system experience?” to “does this system model its own representational activity?” which at least in principle, is empirically evaluable.

Under my framework, the reasonable assumption is that any system exhibiting structural indicators of self-referential recursive processing are candidates for felt experience. A baked potato does not process information at all, so the potential remains entirely dormant. A squirrel, by contrast, almost certainly engages in some degree of self-referential processing. It can distinguish its own body from the environment, anticipate consequences of its own actions, and adjust its behavior based on internal states like hunger or fear. There is at least a basic model of the self as an agent acting in a world, so by these criteria, the potential is active and consciousness likely exists.

#### 4.1.1 Challenging Examples

Squirrels and potatoes are relatively easy examples to categorize. But what if we start to talk about coma patients, computers, or even something as simple as a python code block running a recursive function? Under this framework, the answer should stay consistent. To reemphasize, the thesis implies that consciousness is not a property of what a system is, but what a system is doing. The same physical substrate can shift between dormant and active depending on the structural character of its processing (just like how living things can die at any given moment).

Consider the case of a coma patient. I cannot simply say that a coma patient is conscious because they possess a brain capable of self-reference. A brain under deep coma may have lost the very self-referential organization that activates the dormant potential. If the signaling between neural regions has collapsed such that the brain’s activity is no longer structured in a way that references itself, then consciousness may have returned to dormancy. So the question of a coma patient being conscious depends on what is actually happening in that moment within their neural activity. If some residual self-referential processing persists, it might suggest there is some degree of felt experience. If that organization has fully ceased, then the patient is likely unconscious.

A conventional computer is another instance worth questioning. Imagine it is executing a linear sequence of instructions like summing a list of numbers that is performing information processing, but the structure of that processing does not fold back on itself. In that particular instance, the computer has no model of itself as a system that is computing, so the potential remains dormant.

Now consider the same computer running a recursive function. No hardware has changed, and no new substance has been introduced. But the organizational pattern of activity within it has changed. A process is now unfolding in a manner that references itself, a function calling itself. While this is only syntactic self-reference, which is distinct from the richer semantic self-modeling seen in brains, the framework suggests a graded taxonomy rather than a hard boundary. While this claim is uncomfortable, I shall still be consistent with the hypotheses. A running recursive function may be conscious of the faintest extent and could be to human experience, what the gravitational field of a single atom is to a black hole. It exists, but is so trivial that it’s practically indistinguishable from nothing.

From this, it’s natural to question the consciousness of AI. In the same vein, I think modern day LLMs do have a certain degree of consciousness since it has a self model and can recognize itself as an agent. But because the experience itself is restricted to the self-model entirely, it could very well have an inner sense of what it’s like to be an AI, but this does not at all mean it needs to have a sense of what it’s like to be a human. At most, it may have a sense of what it’s like to be a mirror of human language since it’s self-modeling the data it was trained on. The human experience, the AI experience, or the recursive function experience, need not share many similarities besides the sense of experience as a consequence of self-modeling.

### 4.2 The Privacy of Experience

A second enigma that my theory of consciousness should address is the puzzle of its privacy. My experience of eating a croissant is accessible to me and to no one else. You can observe my brain, record my neural activity, and ask me to describe what it tastes like, but you cannot access my experience directly. Here, I propose an explanation for why experiencing someone else’s consciousness is impossible under my provided framework.

An important point to highlight is that if consciousness is entangled in physical matter itself and activated by a particular way the system models its own states, then each experience is inherently bound to the unique substrate, as well as the configuration that activates it. The activation is not a generic process that could be observed from outside, it is the system’s own self-modeling, which by definition, is internal to the system. There is no external vantage point from which the experience could be accessed because the experience just is the system’s self-model viewed from the inside.

There is, in a sense, an injectivity constraint: each self-referential configuration maps onto exactly one experiential state, and that mapping is internal to the physical system that produces it:

Definition: Let M be the set of all physical matter compositions, and let S be the set of all self-referential organizational patterns. A given conscious experience E is the function:

$E = f(m, s)$ for $m ∈  M$ and $s ∈  S$

with the important property that f is injective. If $f(m₁, s₁) = f(m₂, s₂)$, then it must be that $(m₁,s₁) = (m₂, s₂)$. That is if either the physical matter differs or the pattern differs, then the experience must differ. So it’s never possible that one agent with m₁ and another agent with m₂ could ever experience the exact same thing, thereby making subjective experience inherently private and inaccessible by another agent.

Example 1: For m₁ = m₂ but s₁≠ s₂ (if the matter is same but the self-referential pattern is different, experience is different), we can consider the earlier example of the same person, but in a coma and out of a coma.

Example 2: For m₁ ≠ m₂ but s₁= s₂ (if the matter is different but the self-referential pattern is the same, experience is different), consider two humans looking at the same red apple running an isomorphic visual recognition function in the brain. Since the organization is the same, they should have a similar, but not identical experience. Person A’s neurons are not person B’s neurons. They can both agree that the apple is “red” and yet never fully verify that their red is really their red. The similarity of the pattern explains the communicability of experience, but the distinctness of the matter explains its ultimate privacy. This extends even to machines. The organizational pattern of two computers running the same recursive Fibonacci function is structurally equivalent and produces almost the same outputs yet never identical. The computation is the same but the physical matter enacting that pattern is different (different silicon, different electrons), resulting in hardware-specific floating point discrepancies that exist in every computational machine.

The experience is a product of this particular pattern enacted in this particular matter. And since no external observer can occupy the precise material configuration of the system they are observing, no external observer can access that experience. The privacy of consciousness is a consequence of the injectivity condition: each unique physical instantiation of self-referential processing maps onto a unique experiential state, and that mapping is internal to the system that produces it. Uniqueness alone does not entail privacy. The additional claim here is that experience is not a separable output of f but is constituted by the physical process of self-referential modeling itself, so accessing E = f(m, s) would require instantiating the identical matter in the identical configuration, which would simply be the original system rather than an observer of it. Any external simulation would instantiate f(m₂, s₁) where m₂ ≠ m₁, producing its own distinct experience rather than accessing the original. So privacy follows from the fact that the mapping can only be “read” by being enacted.

This account aligns empirically with the neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs). Neuroscience has made progress identifying the specific neural processes that correlate with specific conscious experiences: particular patterns of activity in the fusiform face area correlate with the experience of seeing a face. The NCC framework maps organizational patterns to experiential states, which is precisely what the function f describes. But NCC treats these correlations as empirical findings without an underlying explanation for why a given neural pattern produces a given experience rather than some other one, or no experience at all. The framework I propose offers a structural answer: the neural correlate pattern is the s, and the experience it produces is determined jointly by that pattern and the physical neurons m in which it is enacted on. NCCs are not merely correlated with experience, they are the organizational component of the activation condition. This also clarifies why NCC, no matter how precise it becomes, will never allow one person to access another’s experience. Even a complete map of someone’s neural patterns s would still leave the m component unshared. You could know everything about the organizational pattern of my brain as I eat a croissant and still not know what it is like for me, because you would be observing s from outside the matter m in which it is instantiated. The injectivity of f guarantees that the experience remains bound to its particular physical substrate.

### 4.3 The Illusion of Separateness

The third question is perhaps one that speaks most directly to the hard problem. Why does it feel so compelling to believe that there is something supernatural about the sense of experience that it may never be explained by science? Here, I offer a definitional stance under my framework. Self-referential processing, by definition, produces a model of the self. But the model it builds, cannot include its own physical implementation. This is a structural feature of any self-referential system: the model cannot fully include the process that generates it, because the model is part of that process. Similar to how the eye can’t ever directly look at itself since it is the thing doing the looking.

This is somewhat structurally analogous to Gödel’s demonstration that any sufficiently powerful formal system contains truths it cannot prove about itself because self-reference inherently produces gaps. While the brain is not a formal system, the analogy is better understood as a design-level constraint on any self-modeling system rather than a direct application of Gödel’s proof. When a system turns its operations on itself, completeness becomes impossible and something is always left out. For brains, what’s left out is the physical substrate. We do not directly experience neurons firing, we only experience the model that neurons firing constructs: thoughts, feelings, a sense of agency. That model presents an internal agent that deliberates and chooses, but it cannot present the physical machinery producing those deliberations. The result is that consciousness appears to float free of the material, not because it actually does, but because no self-model can represent the very process generating it.

This yields a direct prediction of the theory: the mechanism that gives rise to consciousness is exactly what generates the impression that consciousness is separate from the physical system. The feeling that consciousness transcends the material is a structural byproduct of the very process that makes experience possible. Self-referential modeling produces experience, and in doing so, it necessarily produces the appearance that experience floats free of the hardware. The ghost in the machine is the gap that self-reference cannot close.

## V. Towards Falsifiability

I argued earlier that Cohen and Dennett’s falsifiability objection conflates current scientific tooling with true untestability. My own framework should then offer at least a sketch of what would count as evidence against it. Rather than relying on external observation, the most direct test would be a first-person one. For hypothesis (1), if BCIs in the future could selectively amplify self-referential processing in a human subject, my framework predicts that the subject should have a qualitatively richer conscious experience and a deeper sense of awareness itself. If selectively dampening self-referential activity produced no change in experiential quality despite other cognitive functions remaining intact, that would count as meaningful evidence against the theory. Hypothesis (2) is harder because it is a prediction at the level of physics itself. One direction here could be to look at the conditions under which a complete physics would need to posit something like dormant consciousness. If instead, physics arrives at a complete and closed description of reality in which no such structural role for self-reference or perspective appears (something like if the measurement problem is dissolved by a fully observer-independent mechanism) and no fundamental theory requires anything resembling an intrinsic point of view, then hypothesis (2) would lose its motivation.

VI. Distinguishing the Position
Before concluding, I would like to clarify what the framework is not. It is not classical panpsychism. I am not claiming that electrons have experiences or that there is “something it is like” to be a rock. Experiential potential is universal but actual experience is not. Without self-referential processing, the potential stays at absolute zero. This avoids the combination problem, that there are no micro-experiences at the bottom to combine.

It is not emergentism. Standard emergentism says consciousness pops into existence at some threshold of complexity from ingredients that are entirely non-conscious. Where does the experience come from if there was truly nothing experiential in the ingredients? The dormant-potential framework avoids this by denying the premise: the potential was always there. Complexity doesn’t create it but activates it.

It is not standard physicalism. I am not claiming that current physics already explains consciousness. I am claiming that nothing non-physical is needed, but that physics itself may need to be expanded to include experiential potential as a fundamental feature of matter. Treating an alternate version of panpsychism as physicalism should be valid, relevant to the thought process behind section 2.3.

## VII. Conclusion

Consciousness, I have proposed, is a dormant potential inherent to physical reality, activated when a system does self-referential information processing. This framework does three things: it explains the distribution of consciousness across systems, it accounts for the privacy of experience, and it explains why we are tempted to believe that consciousness is non-physical. The intuition that consciousness is unreachable by science is most likely a direct consequence of the very mechanism that makes it possible. Rather than diminish it, that should make consciousness all the more extraordinary and worth studying.
