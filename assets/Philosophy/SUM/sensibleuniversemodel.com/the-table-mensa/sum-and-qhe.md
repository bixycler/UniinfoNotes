 **The Quantum Hall Effect**  is a quantum mechanical phenomenon observed in two-dimensional electron systems (like thin semiconductor layers) subjected to strong perpendicular magnetic fields at very low temperatures. Discovered in 1980 by Klaus von Klitzing (integer QHE) and later extended to fractional cases (FQHE) by Tsui, Stormer, and Laughlin, it manifests as quantized plateaus in the Hall conductivity: σ\_xy = ν (e²/h), where ν is the filling factor (integer for IQHE, fractional like 1/3 or 2/5 for FQHE), e is the electron charge, and h is Planck's constant. Key features include:

-  **Topological robustness** : The quantization is exact and insensitive to impurities or disorder, protected by topology (e.g., Chern numbers in IQHE, anyonic statistics in FQHE).

-  **Edge states and incompressibility** : In FQHE, the bulk is gapped (incompressible quantum fluid), while chiral edge modes carry current.

-  **Anyons in FQHE** : Excitations carry fractional charge (e.g., e/3) and obey fractional statistics, where particle exchanges (braiding) yield phase factors e^{iθ} (0 < θ < 2π), enabling topological quantum computing potential.


This effect bridges condensed matter physics with topology, quantum fluids, and even cosmology (e.g., analogies to black hole horizons).
